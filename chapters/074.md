## 74 — The One Thing Necessary

> *Love grows deepest when it forgets itself and rests entirely in God.*

The contemplative life gradually draws the soul into a profound simplicity. As love deepens, attention becomes less divided. The heart becomes less occupied with itself and more absorbed in God. The ancient writers sometimes described this movement with strong language such as self-annihilation, self-forgetting, or self-hatred. Yet what they were trying to express was not contempt for oneself, but the experience of becoming so captivated by divine love that self-preoccupation quietly falls away.

In ordinary life, much of our attention circles back to ourselves. We think about our needs, our successes, our fears, our reputation, and our desires. Even our spiritual efforts can become subtly self-focused. But in contemplation, the soul begins to loosen its grip on this constant self-reference. Love turns outward and upward. God becomes the center.

This shift is not forced.

It is the natural fruit of love.

The more deeply we love, the less we feel compelled to keep ourselves at the center of every concern.

The author points to the story of Mary and Martha as an image of this mystery. Martha's service was good and necessary. Her care and devotion mattered. Yet Mary chose something different in that moment. She rested in attentive presence.

The contemplative tradition has long seen Mary as a symbol of the soul gathered into God.

Her attention was not scattered.

Her heart was not divided.

She was simply present.

The author goes so far as to suggest that Mary's attention was so fully given to God that even the surrounding activity faded into the background. Whether or not we interpret the story exactly this way, the underlying truth remains powerful: love has the capacity to simplify us. When the heart becomes focused on what matters most, countless lesser concerns lose their urgency.

This does not mean we abandon responsibility.

It means we discover a deeper center from which responsibility can be lived.

The contemplative path is often misunderstood because its fruits are largely hidden. From the outside, it may appear that very little is happening. Yet the author insists that this interior work is among the most fruitful activities a soul can undertake. Love directed toward God becomes a source from which every other virtue draws strength.

Prayer.

Compassion.

Patience.

Humility.

Generosity.

All of them are nourished by love.

The author repeatedly returns to what he calls the "one thing necessary." Beneath the many activities of life, beneath spiritual practices, disciplines, and even theological understanding, there remains a single essential movement: loving God for God's own sake.

This is a striking idea.

Many of us begin our spiritual journey seeking comfort, guidance, healing, wisdom, forgiveness, or peace. These are good desires. Yet over time, love matures. The soul gradually learns to seek God not merely for what God provides, but for who God is.

To love God because God is worthy of love.

To praise God because God is worthy of praise.

To delight in God simply because He is.

This movement carries a unique purity. The more the soul enters it, the more self-interest begins to soften. Love becomes less transactional and more relational. Less concerned with gain and more concerned with communion.

The author also notes that contemplative souls are sometimes misunderstood by others. Throughout history, people devoted to prayer, silence, and inward attentiveness have occasionally been accused of wasting time or neglecting more practical concerns. Yet the contemplative tradition consistently affirms that a life centered in God is never wasted.

Love itself is fruitful.

Even when its fruit cannot immediately be seen.

And so the contemplative learns to trust. Trust that God is at work beneath the surface. Trust that what appears hidden may be accomplishing more than can be measured. Trust that divine providence remains active even when outcomes are unclear.

This trust does not remove every uncertainty.

It does not eliminate every difficulty.

But it allows the soul to rest.

For the one who loves God above all things discovers a remarkable freedom: the freedom to let God be God.

And in that freedom, love finds its deepest home.
