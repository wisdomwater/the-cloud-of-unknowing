## 68 — Losing Yourself in Love

> *The soul becomes most itself when it no longer clings to itself.*

One of the great paradoxes of the contemplative life is that we draw nearer to God not by becoming more absorbed in ourselves, but by gradually becoming less preoccupied with ourselves. The ancient mystics sometimes spoke of this as losing the sense of one's own being. Such language can sound strange to modern ears, yet they were not describing the destruction of the self. They were describing a profound freedom from self-centeredness.

In the beginning of the spiritual journey, much of our attention naturally remains focused on ourselves. We notice our thoughts, our emotions, our successes, our failures, our progress, and our struggles. This is not wrong. It is simply part of being human. Yet over time, as love deepens, the center of gravity slowly shifts. The soul becomes less concerned with itself and more captivated by God.

This transformation happens gradually. No one arrives there overnight. The contemplative path unfolds through countless small acts of surrender, trust, prayer, and faithfulness. Little by little, the heart loosens its grip on its own importance. The constant need to evaluate, measure, and protect the self begins to soften.

As this deepening continues, prayer becomes increasingly simple. The soul discovers moments when it feels less caught in the noise of thought and more settled in a quiet awareness of God's presence. There is less striving. Less self-consciousness. Less effort to make something happen. Instead, there is a growing sense of resting within a reality larger than oneself.

The mystics often struggled to describe this experience because ordinary language is built around separation. We speak of "God" and "me," of "seeker" and "sought," of "lover" and "Beloved." Yet in the deepest moments of contemplation, these distinctions seem less important than they once did. The soul remains fully itself, yet feels held within a Presence so vast and intimate that self-awareness fades into the background.

This is what the ancient writers meant by abstraction. The soul is not escaping the body or rejecting the world. It is becoming less entangled in the endless stream of self-reference that ordinarily occupies the mind. Attention is gathered and simplified. The heart rests more fully in God.

The journey toward such simplicity often takes many years. The author compares it to a long ascent up a mountain. At first the climb feels difficult and steep. Progress may seem painfully slow. Yet each faithful step changes something within us. Every act of love, every prayer of surrender, every return after wandering helps prepare the soul for deeper stillness.

Sometimes God accelerates this journey through extraordinary moments of grace. There may be seasons when a person experiences a profound awakening, a sudden clarity, or a deep interior transformation that accomplishes in a short time what otherwise might have taken years. These moments are gifts, not achievements. They cannot be manufactured or controlled. They arrive as grace and leave their lasting imprint upon the soul.

Yet the author is careful to remind us that such experiences are not required. Many souls travel the path more quietly. They continue faithfully in prayer, surrender, patience, and love. Their growth may appear slower, yet God is no less present within it. The destination remains the same: a heart increasingly free to rest in Him.

The deeper the soul enters this way of love, the less it feels confined by its own narrow perspective. It begins to sense life from within a larger horizon. Personal concerns do not disappear, but they no longer dominate everything. The self remains, yet it is no longer the center around which all things revolve.

The mystics sometimes described this as dwelling in God. Not because they ceased to exist, but because they discovered that their truest life had always been rooted in something greater than themselves. What once felt like losing themselves became the discovery of a deeper identity hidden within divine love.

The contemplative journey is not the erasure of the self.

It is the gentle liberation of the self from its own smallness.

And in that freedom, the soul learns to live more fully, love more deeply, and rest more completely in God.
