## 75 — Trusting the God Who Calls

> *Where God truly leads, God also sustains.*

One of the deepest fears on the spiritual path is the fear of letting go. We worry that if we trust God more fully, something essential will be lost. We wonder who will care for us, provide for us, guide us, or protect us. The mind naturally seeks certainty before it is willing to surrender.

Yet the contemplative tradition repeatedly returns to a different wisdom: when God genuinely calls a soul, He also provides what that soul needs.

This does not mean life becomes easy.

It does not mean suffering disappears.

It does not mean every question receives an immediate answer.

But it does mean that grace accompanies every authentic call.

The author speaks boldly about divine providence. If God invites a person into a deeper life of prayer, God will also provide the strength needed for that calling. If abundance is given, it may be received with gratitude. If scarcity comes, God can provide patience, endurance, and contentment. The true gift is not always the removal of difficulty but the presence that carries us through it.

This teaching does not encourage recklessness. The author repeatedly emphasizes the importance of discernment. Not every impulse is a divine invitation. Not every desire comes from God. The contemplative life requires honesty, wisdom, and careful listening.

A genuine call produces peace, even when it asks something difficult.

A false call often produces confusion, pride, or self-importance.

The soul must learn to listen carefully.

And yet once a call has been discerned, trust becomes essential.

Many spiritual seekers spend years waiting for guarantees that never arrive. They want complete certainty before taking a single step. But love rarely works that way. Love moves forward with trust. It learns to place its future into hands larger than its own.

The author then returns to an important distinction that runs throughout the Cloud of Unknowing. There are moments when the soul actively reaches toward God through prayer, longing, and love. This is the work the book primarily describes. Yet there are also moments when God takes the initiative in a more direct way, drawing the soul beyond its own efforts into a deeper experience of grace.

The contemplative cannot manufacture these moments.

They arrive as gifts.

The soul's task is not to control them but to remain available.

For most of us, the path consists largely of ordinary faithfulness. We pray. We return. We surrender. We love as best we can. Sometimes God grants extraordinary moments of clarity or intimacy. Often He works more quietly, shaping the heart through steady devotion.

The author also reminds us that not every soul follows the same path. Some people are naturally drawn toward contemplation. Others express their love for God primarily through service, action, caregiving, teaching, craftsmanship, hospitality, or countless other forms of faithful living.

This diversity is not a flaw.

It is part of God's creativity.

Not every flower blooms in the same way.

Not every soul is called to the same expression of holiness.

What matters is not comparing our path to another's. What matters is responding faithfully to the grace we have been given.

The contemplative life is not reserved for spiritual elites. Yet neither can it be forced. The deepest forms of contemplative prayer arise from invitation, not ambition. They emerge naturally when love has matured enough to seek God for God's own sake.

The author warns against two extremes. One is remaining spiritually asleep, never seriously pursuing either prayer or transformation. The other is attempting to force ourselves into experiences for which we are not yet prepared. Wisdom walks a middle path.

Be faithful where you are.

Remain open to where God may lead.

Trust the pace of grace.

Finally, the author returns once more to the great mystery at the heart of the contemplative path: no matter how deeply we encounter God in this life, God always remains beyond our complete understanding.

The cloud of unknowing never fully disappears.

The mystery remains.

The Infinite remains infinite.

Yet this is not a cause for discouragement.

It is an invitation to wonder.

For the goal of contemplation is not to possess God.

It is to be possessed by love.
