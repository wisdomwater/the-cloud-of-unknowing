## 54 — When Words Fall Silent

> *The closer we come to God, the fewer words we need.*

The spiritual journey often begins with learning.

We gather teachings, study sacred writings, reflect upon divine mysteries, and seek language capable of expressing what stirs within us. Words become companions along the path. They help us orient ourselves toward God and awaken our desire for deeper communion.

And this is good.

Every true word about God is a gift.

Yet there comes a point when words can no longer carry us where love longs to go.

The ancient writer observes that the higher we ascend into divine mystery, the fewer words seem necessary. At first, there is much to say. We describe God's goodness, wisdom, power, beauty, mercy, and love. We explore symbols, stories, and sacred images. We fill books with reflections because the heart is trying to speak about what it has glimpsed.

But as the soul draws nearer to the mystery itself, language begins to grow thin.

Not because God becomes less real.

But because God becomes more real.

The intellect discovers that the Infinite cannot be contained within its categories. The heart senses a depth that no explanation can adequately describe. The soul begins to understand why the greatest contemplatives so often spoke of silence.

Silence is not the absence of truth.

It is the reverent response to a truth too vast for speech.

The ancient writer notes that spiritual teaching often moves in two directions. Sometimes we speak about God through affirmation. We call God good, wise, loving, beautiful, and holy. These names are true because all goodness, wisdom, love, beauty, and holiness have their source in Him.

Yet there is another movement.

We learn to release even these affirmations.

Not because they are false, but because God is more than what we mean when we use those words.

God is not merely good as we understand goodness.

God is not merely wise as we understand wisdom.

God is not merely loving as we understand love.

Every true name points toward Him, yet none can fully contain Him.

This is why the contemplative path becomes a path of holy unknowing.

At first, we gather concepts.

Later, we loosen our grip on them.

At first, we speak.

Later, we listen.

At first, we seek understanding.

Later, we rest in wonder.

The soul slowly learns that God is not reached through accumulating more and more ideas. Instead, we become willing to release what is partial in order to receive what is whole.

The ancient writer explains that when we speak positively about God, we naturally begin with the highest and most beautiful qualities we know. But when we enter the path of unknowing, we begin by letting go of the things that are furthest from God's true nature.

We release crude images.

We release false assumptions.

We release distortions born of fear, ego, and limitation.

Then we continue letting go.

Not because God is absent from these things, but because God is infinitely greater than them.

The movement is like climbing a mountain through clouds.

Each step leaves something behind. Familiar landmarks disappear. Certainty gives way to mystery. Yet with every release, the horizon widens.

And eventually, there comes a place where the soul stands before a mystery that words can no longer illuminate.

There is no argument.

No explanation.

No conclusion.

Only presence.

Only wonder.

Only love.

This is not the failure of understanding.

It is its fulfillment.

For the deepest knowledge of God is not information about Him, but participation in Him. The soul becomes united to what it cannot fully describe. It discovers that the silence beyond words is not empty at all.

It is filled with God.
