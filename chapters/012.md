## 12 — The Heart That Refuses Lesser Comforts

> *The soul begins to change when nothing less than God can satisfy its hunger.*

There comes a season in the contemplative life when the heart slowly loses interest in lesser comforts. This does not happen because creation has become meaningless, nor because relationships, beauty, work, or ordinary joys no longer matter. In many ways, these things become even more precious. But beneath them all, something deeper begins awakening within the soul—a longing that cannot fully rest in anything temporary.

At first this longing can feel confusing. You may try to soothe it with distractions, accomplishments, spiritual ideas, endless learning, or the approval of others. Sometimes you may even attempt to fill it with religious activity itself, hoping that more effort or more understanding will quiet the ache. But eventually the soul begins to recognize that nothing created can completely hold the longing it carries for God.

And this realization is both painful and beautiful.

Painful, because many things that once satisfied you no longer seem able to reach the deepest places within. Beautiful, because the heart is slowly being gathered toward what is eternal. Something within you is learning to seek beneath every surface experience for the hidden Presence that quietly sustains all things.

This is the love the ancient contemplatives spoke about with such tenderness. A love that cannot fully settle for substitutes. A love that keeps searching beneath appearances for the Beloved Himself. Like Mary Magdalene weeping beside the empty tomb, the soul reaches toward something no earthly comfort can replace. Even the voices of angels could not distract her longing. Only Christ Himself would do.

This is not a rejection of the world. It is the soul becoming honest about what it most deeply desires.

Many people experience moments like this without fully understanding what is happening within them. They feel restless in the middle of success. Lonely in the midst of noise. Spiritually thirsty while surrounded by information, entertainment, or achievement. Something inside them continues whispering that there must be more than endless consumption, striving, and distraction.

The contemplative path does not ask you to run from this ache. It invites you to listen to it.

For beneath the ache is love itself. A holy homesickness. A quiet remembering that you were made for communion deeper than thought alone can provide. The longing itself becomes a kind of prayer, drawing the soul gently back toward the One from whom it came.

Because this hunger is so inward and hidden, others may not always understand it. Some may think you are becoming too quiet, too detached, or insufficiently ambitious. Others may misunderstand the simplicity of your life or the hiddenness of your prayer. But contemplation slowly teaches you not to build your life around defending yourself.

You do not need to interrupt your inward turning every time someone misunderstands it. You do not need to endlessly justify your longing for silence, stillness, or simplicity. Over time, love itself becomes your explanation.

And alongside this deepening longing, something else quietly begins to grow within the soul: trust.

Not naïve certainty. Not denial of suffering or difficulty. But a quieter trust that God is able to sustain what God calls. The contemplative path often feels uncertain because it loosens the illusion of control. The soul slowly releases its exhausting attempts to secure itself completely before surrendering to love, and this can feel frightening to the anxious mind that longs for guarantees before it can rest.

But little by little, you begin discovering that grace often arrives before you understand how everything will unfold. Strength appears for the moment that requires it. Provision comes in unexpected ways. Patience quietly deepens within hardship. And even when life remains imperfect, you begin realizing that you are being carried more than you once believed.

This does not mean abandoning wisdom or responsibility. It simply means releasing the exhausting belief that your survival depends entirely upon your own striving and grasping. The contemplative life slowly loosens the clenched fist.

And in that loosening, humility begins to bloom.

Not humiliation. Not self-rejection. But a softer awareness of your smallness within the vastness of God. A willingness to rest beneath mystery rather than trying to master it. And strangely, this humility becomes peaceful, because when you no longer believe you must hold the whole world together, the soul can finally breathe again.
