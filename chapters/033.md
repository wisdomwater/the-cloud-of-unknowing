## 33 — The Holy Darkness Beyond Understanding

> *The soul draws nearest to God when it releases the need to possess Him through certainty.*

The contemplative path eventually leads the soul into a strange and holy place — a place where the ordinary ways of understanding no longer seem sufficient. The ancient writer speaks of it with unusual language: nothing, nowhere, a cloud of unknowing. At first these words can sound unsettling, even frightening. The mind naturally longs for clarity, direction, explanation, and certainty. It wants something stable to hold onto, something it can define, organize, and understand.

But God cannot be reduced into an object the mind controls. The deeper a person moves into contemplative prayer, the more they begin realizing that every image, every concept, and every explanation eventually reaches its limit. Human understanding can point toward the Divine, but it cannot contain the fullness of the Divine itself. And beyond the edges of understanding, the soul begins encountering mystery. Not emptiness. Not absence. But a living fullness too vast for the mind to fully hold.

There is something both beautiful and difficult here: spiritually speaking, nowhere bodily becomes everywhere spiritually. In other words, when the mind finally releases its need to locate God as though He were merely an object somewhere far away, the soul slowly awakens to a Presence that cannot be confined by place at all.

This is why contemplative prayer can initially feel like entering darkness. Not because God has disappeared. But because the ordinary ways we navigate reality begin falling quiet.

The senses cannot fully grasp this Presence. The imagination cannot properly picture it. The intellect cannot master it through analysis or explanation. And so the ordinary self — the part of us accustomed to control and certainty — begins feeling disoriented.

The writer even says that the outward self calls this experience “nothing,” because the ego struggles to value what it cannot dominate or possess. Yet the deeper soul recognizes something entirely different unfolding beneath the silence. Not nothing. Everything.

For beneath the quiet, the soul slowly encounters a reality deeper than ordinary consciousness itself — a Presence hidden beneath the constant movement of thought, fear, striving, and distraction.

At first, however, this encounter can feel painful. When people enter silence honestly, they often begin seeing themselves more clearly than before. Old wounds rise into awareness. Hidden fears reveal themselves. Regrets, compulsions, shame, pride, grief, and restless desires begin surfacing from places long covered over by noise and activity. The silence becomes like a mirror. Not condemning. Simply revealing.

And many people turn away here. Not because contemplation has failed, but because they are encountering themselves without distraction for perhaps the first time.

The ancient writer describes this season almost like an inward purging of the heart. Some experience dryness. Some feel sorrow. Some feel temporarily abandoned or discouraged because they expected spiritual sweetness and instead discovered inner conflict and vulnerability. Yet the contemplative tradition gently reminds us that this too belongs to the path. Not punishment. Purification. The slow uncovering of the heart before Love.

And if the soul remains patient — neither forcing nor fleeing, neither grasping for quick reassurance nor collapsing into despair — something slowly begins changing within. The heaviness softens. The compulsive noise loses some of its grip. Small moments of peace begin quietly appearing between the thoughts. Not because the soul has finally explained God. But because it has begun surrendering the need to.

This is one of the great paradoxes hidden within contemplation: the closer the soul draws toward God, the more it realizes God cannot be fully comprehended. And strangely, this realization no longer feels threatening. It becomes freeing. The soul no longer needs to imprison mystery inside certainty in order to trust it.

The writer says the deepest knowledge of God comes through unknowing. Not ignorance in the ordinary sense, but a sacred humility that releases the need to reduce the Infinite into concepts small enough for the mind to manage.

It is like standing beneath the night sky in silence. Like listening to music too beautiful to fully explain. Like sitting quietly beside someone deeply loved.

You do not possess the mystery. You consent to it. And within that surrender, the soul begins discovering another kind of vision entirely. Not sight through the eyes. Not certainty through the intellect. But a quiet communion born within love itself.
