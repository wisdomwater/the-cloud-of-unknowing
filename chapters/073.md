## 73 — Love Knows What the Mind Cannot

> *The heart can rest in God long before the mind understands Him.*

The contemplative path eventually reaches a point where thinking alone can no longer carry the soul forward. Reflection, meditation, imagination, and study all have their place. They prepare the heart, deepen understanding, and awaken desire for God. Yet there comes a season when the soul is invited beyond these supports into a simpler way of relating to the Divine.

This invitation should never be rushed. The imagination is not an enemy of prayer. Sacred stories, images from Scripture, reflections on Christ's life, and thoughtful meditation are precious gifts. They teach the soul how to seek God. They cultivate faithfulness. They prepare the ground for deeper prayer.

But eventually the heart learns something remarkable.

God is not an idea.

God is not an image.

God is not even the sum of all our spiritual understanding.

God is infinitely more.

The author warns that no one should attempt to leap prematurely into contemplative prayer. The soul must first be formed through prayer, reflection, and ordinary faithfulness. Only when God gently draws the heart beyond these things can it safely enter a simpler way of loving.

When that invitation comes, prayer begins to shift. The soul becomes less concerned with gathering thoughts about God and more concerned with remaining turned toward Him. Love becomes simpler. Attention becomes quieter. The heart learns to rest without needing to constantly think.

This is why the author says that love can reach where knowledge cannot.

Our understanding has limits.

Love does not.

In this life we can know many things about God. We can learn theology, study Scripture, reflect on spiritual truths, and contemplate creation. Yet God Himself always remains beyond the grasp of the mind. No thought can contain Him. No definition can capture Him.

Love, however, is able to enter where concepts stop.

Not because it understands more, but because it trusts more.

The contemplative life is built upon this trust. The soul learns to remain present before a mystery it cannot solve. Rather than demanding certainty, it becomes willing to dwell within reverent unknowing. This is not intellectual laziness. It is humility. The recognition that God is greater than our understanding is itself a form of wisdom.

The author also reflects on the power of a settled intention. Every spiritual life is shaped by the direction in which the heart is pointed. A person who has sincerely chosen God and continues returning to that choice is being quietly transformed, even when progress feels slow or invisible.

The intention to love God matters.

The intention to seek God matters.

The intention to return after wandering matters.

These repeated choices gradually reshape the soul.

This is why the author suggests that a person rooted in a sincere desire for God often experiences life differently than one whose heart remains divided. The direction of the heart influences everything else. Even imperfections and failures are encountered differently when the soul's deepest desire remains turned toward God.

The chapter then turns toward humility, which the author calls meekness. He suggests there are two ways humility grows. The first comes through reflection on our limitations, weaknesses, and dependence upon God. Such reflection can be helpful, especially in the early stages of the spiritual life.

Yet there is a deeper humility.

This deeper humility does not arise from focusing on ourselves at all.

It arises from focusing on God.

When the soul becomes absorbed in divine love, self-preoccupation naturally fades. We no longer need to convince ourselves that we are small because we are already standing before a greatness that gently places everything in perspective. Humility emerges not from self-condemnation but from wonder.

This is why true humility feels spacious rather than heavy.

It is not humiliation.

It is not self-rejection.

It is the freedom that comes from no longer needing to place ourselves at the center of the story.

For a brief moment, while resting deeply in God, the soul may lose awareness of its own importance altogether. There is only love. Only presence. Only the quiet recognition that everything is gift.

Such moments rarely last.

Yet they leave their mark.

And each time the soul returns to love, humility grows a little deeper.
