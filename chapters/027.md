## 27 — The Fire That Judges Others

> *Love does not grow*
>
> *through superiority.*
>
> *The humble heart learns compassion.*

There is a kind of spiritual passion that looks holy on the surface but is quietly fueled by fear, pride, or inner unrest.

The person becomes consumed with correcting others.

Condemning others.

Fixing others.

They carry constant outrage toward the failures, sins, blindness, or imperfections they see around them. And often they sincerely believe this intensity comes from love for truth or devotion to God.

But the ancient contemplatives urge caution here.

For the ego can hide itself even inside religious zeal.

Sometimes people who feel inwardly unsettled try to regain certainty by becoming judges of everyone else. Focusing on the faults of others temporarily distracts the mind from its own confusion, wounds, insecurity, or unfinished inner work.

And because of this, spiritual judgment can become strangely intoxicating.

It creates the illusion of clarity.

The illusion of superiority.

The illusion of righteousness.

But rarely does it create peace.

The contemplative path moves in another direction.

Not toward moral indifference.

Not toward pretending evil does not exist.

But toward humility.

Toward discernment wrapped in compassion.

Toward remembering how limited our own understanding often is.

The ancient writer speaks symbolically about people who lose the ability to distinguish wisely between things — who react quickly, judge harshly, and speak with certainty before understanding deeply. Though the imagery comes from another time, the insight remains profoundly relevant.

The reactive mind simplifies people too quickly.

It divides the world into heroes and enemies.

Pure and impure.

Awakened and blind.

Worthy and unworthy.

But contemplative prayer slowly softens this habit.

Not because truth disappears.

But because love teaches us how complex human beings truly are.

Every person carries wounds you cannot fully see.

Every soul struggles with hidden fears, contradictions, longings, conditioning, and pain.

And once you begin seeing this clearly within yourself, judgment toward others often becomes quieter.

More patient.

More merciful.

The soul becomes less interested in proving itself right and more interested in becoming compassionate.

This is one of the hidden fruits of genuine contemplation.

It humbles certainty.

Not into confusion.

But into tenderness.

The contemplative does not rush to condemn because they know how much of their own heart still remains unfinished. They understand how easy it is for fear, ego, trauma, insecurity, or spiritual ambition to disguise itself as truth.

And this awareness creates gentleness.

Not weakness.

Not passivity.

But wise restraint.

The ancient writer also warns against becoming consumed with spiritual pride disguised as special insight. Some people begin trusting their own opinions so completely that they lose touch with grounded wisdom, wise counsel, humility, community, and the slow discernment that protects the soul.

This danger still exists today.

Especially in spiritual spaces filled with certainty, charisma, emotional intensity, or claims of secret knowledge.

But contemplative prayer continually returns the soul to simplicity.

To groundedness.

To humility.

To love.

You do not need to become the judge of everyone around you.

You do not need to carry the burden of correcting every soul you encounter.

You do not need to prove your spiritual clarity through outrage.

The deepest saints are often marked less by harshness than by compassion.

Less by superiority than by mercy.

Less by frantic certainty than by quiet wisdom.

For the closer the soul moves toward God, the more clearly it begins seeing that every human being is fragile, unfinished, beloved, and in need of grace.

Including you.

And in that shared human tenderness, judgment slowly loosens its grip.

## Breath Prayer

> Breath in: *Soften my harshness*
>
> Breath out: *Teach me compassionate wisdom*
