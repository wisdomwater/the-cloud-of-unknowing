## 27 — The Fire That Judges Others

> *Love does not grow through superiority.*

There is a kind of spiritual intensity that appears holy on the surface while quietly being sustained by fear, pride, or inner unrest beneath. A person becomes consumed with correcting others, condemning others, or exposing the blindness and failures they see around them. Often they sincerely believe this urgency is proof of their devotion to truth or faithfulness to God.

But the ancient contemplatives urge great caution here. For the ego is capable of hiding itself even within religious zeal. Sometimes when the soul feels uncertain, wounded, or inwardly unsettled, it reaches for judgment as a way to regain control. Focusing on the faults of others can temporarily distract us from our own confusion, insecurity, grief, or unfinished inner work. And because of this, spiritual judgment can become strangely intoxicating. It creates the illusion of certainty, superiority, and righteousness while leaving the heart increasingly restless underneath.

Yet the contemplative path moves in another direction. Not toward moral indifference or the denial of truth, but toward humility. Toward discernment wrapped carefully in compassion. Toward remembering how limited our own understanding often is, even when we feel most certain.

The ancient writer speaks symbolically about those who lose the ability to see wisely — people who react quickly, judge harshly, and speak with certainty before understanding deeply. Though the imagery belongs to another age, the insight remains painfully relevant. The reactive mind simplifies human beings too quickly. It divides the world into categories of pure and impure, awakened and blind, worthy and unworthy.

But contemplation slowly softens this habit within the soul. Not because truth disappears, but because love teaches us how complex human beings truly are. Every person carries wounds you cannot fully see. Every soul struggles with hidden fears, contradictions, conditioning, longings, and pain. And once you begin encountering these realities honestly within yourself, judgment toward others often becomes quieter and more patient.

The soul becomes less interested in proving itself right and more interested in becoming compassionate.

This is one of the hidden fruits of genuine contemplation. It humbles certainty — not into confusion, but into tenderness. The contemplative does not rush to condemn because they know how unfinished their own heart still is. They understand how easily fear, insecurity, ego, trauma, or spiritual ambition can disguise itself as truth.

And this awareness creates gentleness. Not weakness. Not passivity. But wise restraint born from humility.

The ancient contemplatives also warned about spiritual pride disguised as special insight. Some people become so convinced of their own clarity that they slowly lose touch with grounded wisdom, wise counsel, humility, and the patient discernment that protects the soul. This danger still exists today, especially in spiritual spaces shaped by emotional intensity, charisma, outrage, or claims of secret knowledge.

But contemplative prayer keeps returning the heart to something simpler.

To groundedness. To humility. To love.

You do not need to become the judge of every person around you. You do not need to carry the exhausting burden of correcting every soul you encounter. And you do not need outrage in order to prove your spiritual sincerity.

The deepest saints are rarely marked by harshness. More often they are recognized by compassion, mercy, patience, and quiet wisdom. For the closer the soul moves toward God, the more clearly it begins seeing that every human being is fragile, unfinished, beloved, and deeply in need of grace.

Including you.

And within that shared tenderness, judgment slowly begins to loosen its grip.

### Breath Prayer

> **Inhale:** *Soften my harshness*
>
> **Exhale:** *Teach me compassionate wisdom*
