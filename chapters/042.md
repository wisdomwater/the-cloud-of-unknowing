## 42 — Losing Yourself in Love

> *The soul becomes whole not by possessing God, but by surrendering into Him.*

There are some realities in the spiritual life that cannot truly be explained. They may be spoken about. Pointed toward. Hinted at through symbols and language. But the experience itself remains beyond the reach of ordinary speech.

The ancient contemplatives understood this well. They knew that divine union could never be reduced to ideas, arguments, or descriptions. Words may circle around the mystery, but they cannot contain it. The deepest movements of God within the soul belong to a silence more intimate than language.

And yet, out of love, the saints still tried to speak of these things—not to satisfy curiosity, but to gently guide the seeking heart.

The perfection of the soul, the writer says, is nothing less than a profound oneness between God and the human person through love. Not sameness. Not the erasure of personhood. But a union so deep that the soul becomes fully aligned with divine charity. The heart begins to rest in God the way a branch rests in the vine or a child rests safely in the arms of a loving parent.

This union is too deep to be fully understood by the intellect alone. But its signs become visible.

Where humility grows, where compassion deepens, where self-centeredness softens, where peace slowly takes root, the presence of this hidden union begins to shine quietly through a person’s life. The virtues are not separate achievements added onto contemplation. They are the natural fruit of a soul slowly being gathered into love.

This contemplative path is not merely a technique of prayer. It is the gradual emptying of the self before God.

The ancient writer calls it a “cloud of unknowing,” a hidden love wrapped in purity and silence. It is the place where thoughts begin loosening their grip and the soul learns to rest in God without needing constant explanation or emotional certainty. Prayer becomes simpler there. Fewer words are needed. The heart learns to remain quietly turned toward the Divine like a flower opening toward sunlight. And in this silence, something deeper is revealed.

You begin learning not only to release your attachment to the world, but also your attachment to the self you spend so much energy protecting. Christ’s invitation to deny yourself is not a call toward self-hatred or harshness. It is an invitation to loosen the false identities, compulsions, fears, and grasping that keep the soul from resting freely in God.

Grace leads where effort alone cannot go. You cannot force yourself into divine union through willpower or spiritual ambition. Contemplation is always first the work of God within you. Your role is not to manufacture holiness, but to consent gently to grace as it moves. The soul learns slowly how to become receptive—open, surrendered, attentive, and willing to be carried. This is why humility matters so deeply on the contemplative path.

The mind constantly wants guarantees. It wants proof that the path is working. It wants spiritual experiences it can measure and possess. But contemplation asks something more vulnerable of you. It asks you to continue walking even when you cannot fully explain what is unfolding within. Love matures in trust. And over time, the soul discovers that God Himself has been the One drawing it all along.

The writer then speaks of a deeper surrender still: learning to forget even the constant awareness of yourself in order to become more fully attentive to God. At first, the soul begins simply by noticing its own existence before God—becoming aware of its inner life, its longings, its need for grace. But gradually, as love deepens, even this self-awareness softens and gives way to a more direct resting in the Divine Presence itself.

This is not the destruction of the self. It is the healing of self-preoccupation. The soul becomes less fascinated with its own spiritual state and more absorbed in love itself. Like two people gazing at one another so deeply that self-consciousness fades, the contemplative heart slowly forgets itself in adoration.

This is the hidden work of love. And no one can fully understand it except the one who has begun to taste it for themselves.
