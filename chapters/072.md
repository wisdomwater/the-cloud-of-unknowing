## 72 — The Continual Turning of the Heart

> *The spiritual life is not measured by extraordinary experiences, but by the heart's continual return to God.*

The contemplative path is, at its core, a life of continual turning. Again and again, the soul lifts itself toward God. Sometimes this movement is conscious and deliberate. Sometimes it arises so quietly and naturally that it feels almost effortless. Yet whether the movement is strong or weak, clear or hidden, the heart's turning toward God remains the essence of the journey.

The author reflects on humanity's original calling. Before the fragmentation and distractions of ordinary life, human beings were created for communion with God. The deepest purpose of the soul was not endless activity, anxiety, or self-concern, but loving union with its Creator. The contemplative life is, in many ways, a gradual return to that original orientation.

Of course, we do not live in that original simplicity. We carry limitations, distractions, wounds, responsibilities, and the frailty of our human condition. The body grows tired. The mind wanders. Attention scatters. No one remains perfectly recollected at all times. Yet grace continually invites us back.

The important thing is not perfection.

The important thing is return.

Each time the heart remembers God, it has already begun the journey home again.

This is why the author emphasizes discretion and wisdom. The spiritual life is not a matter of forcing ourselves beyond our limits. Love grows best when it remains grounded in humility. We are called to respond faithfully to the grace we have been given, not to compare ourselves with others or demand from ourselves what God has not asked.

Some souls are given great capacities for prayer. Others are asked to love God amid demanding circumstances, ordinary responsibilities, or physical limitations. Each person must learn the rhythm of grace in their own life. The goal is not to imitate another's experience but to cooperate with God's invitation in the present moment.

As the contemplative life deepens, these movements toward God often become increasingly simple. The author compares them to sparks rising from a fire. They appear suddenly, without calculation or elaborate preparation. A brief turning of the heart. A moment of surrender. A silent act of love. A wordless awareness of God's presence.

These movements arise not from complicated reasoning but from a deeper interior attraction. Love begins to move more quickly than thought. The soul no longer needs to construct elaborate pathways toward God because its deepest desire has already learned the direction.

Such moments may be brief.

Yet they are precious.

A single sincere movement of love can carry the heart closer to God than many restless thoughts.

The author also returns to the image of forgetting creatures—not in the sense of rejecting the world, but in the sense of refusing to become trapped by it. To forget creatures is to release our fixation upon them. It is to stop treating temporary things as ultimate things. The soul learns to appreciate the gifts of creation without allowing them to replace the Creator.

This movement is not hostile to the world.

It is what allows us to love the world rightly.

When God becomes our center, everything else finds its proper place.

The author reminds us that this simplicity of love does not arise at the beginning of the journey. Most people first learn through meditation, reflection, imagination, sacred stories, and deliberate prayer. These are good and necessary teachers. They prepare the heart. They awaken desire. They cultivate faithfulness.

Only later, for some souls, does prayer become simpler and more direct.

Even then, meditation remains a gift rather than something to despise. Every stage of the journey has its purpose. The seed is not inferior to the flower. The flower is not superior to the seed. Each belongs to the unfolding life of the plant.

So it is with prayer.

God meets us in meditation.

God meets us in reflection.

God meets us in silence.

God meets us in longing.

The goal is not to reach a more advanced technique but to become more available to love.

And love, once awakened, spends a lifetime learning how to return.
