## 69 — The Humility of Not Being the Center

> *True humility is not thinking less of yourself, but resting in something greater than yourself.*

The mystics often spoke of discovering their own nothingness, a phrase that can sound severe or unsettling to modern ears. Yet they were not teaching self-hatred or denying the goodness of human life. They were describing a profound spiritual realization: that our existence is not self-sustaining. Every breath, every moment, every gift of life flows from the One who is the source of all being.

Most of us can understand this intellectually. We know that we did not create ourselves. We recognize that our lives depend upon countless gifts beyond our control. Such knowledge is valuable, and it can help soften pride. Yet the mystics distinguish between knowing this truth and feeling it.

To know that we depend upon God is one thing.

To experience that dependence directly is another.

The deeper realization comes not through analysis but through love. As the soul grows in prayer, it gradually becomes less absorbed in itself and more attentive to God. The constant feeling of being the center of everything begins to loosen. What emerges is not self-contempt but freedom. The soul discovers that it does not need to carry the weight of being its own foundation.

This is what the mystics called humility.

Humility is not humiliation.

It is not the denial of one's worth.

It is the joyful recognition that our lives are rooted in a reality infinitely larger than ourselves.

As this awareness deepens, prayer becomes increasingly simple. During moments of contemplation, the ordinary sense of self can fade into the background. Attention becomes gathered, quiet, and spacious. The soul rests more fully in God than in its own thoughts, concerns, or self-image.

Yet this experience is not permanent. Even the most mature contemplatives return to ordinary awareness. They continue to live in the world, make decisions, care for others, and navigate the challenges of daily life. The mystical state is not an escape from humanity but a glimpse into a deeper dimension of it.

The author notes that spiritual growth often unfolds in cycles. There are seasons of greater clarity and seasons of greater obscurity. Times when the soul feels close to God and times when it feels distant. Times of ascent and times of descent.

This rhythm is not evidence of failure.

It is part of the journey itself.

Again and again, the soul is invited upward into greater trust. Again and again, it returns to ordinary life carrying a little more wisdom, a little more tenderness, and a little more freedom than before. Each cycle deepens the work. Each return becomes another opportunity to practice love.

The mystics used many words for this process: abstraction, transcendence, forgetting oneself, surrender. Whatever language is chosen, the reality points toward the same mystery. The soul gradually learns to release its tight grip on itself so that it may rest more fully in God.

As this happens, many virtues begin to flower naturally. Patience becomes less forced. Compassion grows more instinctive. Defensiveness softens. The need to constantly prove oneself begins to fade. These changes arise not because a person is trying harder to be virtuous, but because love is quietly reshaping the heart from within.

The author even suggests that what we often call humility before this transformation may be only the beginning of humility. Much of what appears humble can still be mixed with self-concern, fear, or subtle pride. True humility emerges when the self no longer occupies center stage.

And yet this humility is wonderfully gentle.

It does not diminish us.

It liberates us.

For when we no longer need to be the center of everything, we become free to participate in something far greater than ourselves.

The introduction closes with a reminder that all of these teachings ultimately point toward one purpose: helping the soul grow in love. The language may at times feel mysterious. The concepts may stretch beyond ordinary understanding. Yet the goal remains beautifully simple.

To love God.

To surrender into grace.

To become increasingly available to the Presence that has always been sustaining us.

And whenever words become too complicated, love remains the clearest guide.
