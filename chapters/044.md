## 44 — The Doorway and the Call

> *Walk the path given to you, and trust God with the rest.*

The contemplative path does not begin in extraordinary experiences.

It begins in a doorway.

The ancient writer reminds us that Christ Himself is that doorway. Through His humanity, His compassion, His suffering, and His love, we are invited into the mystery of God. This is not a secret path reserved for a spiritual elite. It is a plain and open way available to every soul willing to enter with humility.

Many people spend their lives standing at this doorway, contemplating the love of Christ, reflecting on His life, His mercy, and His sacrifice. They find nourishment there. They discover healing there. They grow in devotion, gratitude, and trust. And this alone is a beautiful and holy path.

There is no shame in remaining near the door.

The writer is careful to say that those who faithfully meditate on Christ and open their hearts to His love will find more than enough spiritual food for the journey. God does not measure souls by how far inward they travel, but by how faithfully they respond to the grace they have been given.

The danger comes when we become impatient.

The mind longs for spiritual achievement. It wants special experiences. It wants to bypass the ordinary path and climb directly into the heights of contemplation through clever ideas, unusual techniques, or spiritual ambition. Yet the soul that tries to force its way into mysteries before it has learned humility often becomes lost in illusion.

The contemplative life is not conquered.

It is received.

Love cannot be seized through effort. God cannot be manipulated through spiritual skill. The deepest realities of prayer remain gifts.

This is why the ancient teachers continually emphasize simplicity, patience, and trust. The soul must first learn how to walk before it learns how to fly. It must become rooted before it reaches upward. The humble path may seem slow, but it is also safe.

And when the time is right, God Himself becomes the guide.

The writer speaks of a quiet inner drawing—a gentle increase of longing for God that grows over time. Not excitement. Not spiritual restlessness. Not the desire to be special. But a steady, deepening hunger for divine presence. The soul begins sensing that there is more to discover, more surrender to embrace, more love waiting beneath the surface of ordinary prayer.

This calling cannot be manufactured.

Nor can it be judged from the outside.

Some souls are drawn toward deeper contemplative silence. Others are called primarily into lives of service, action, family, teaching, or mercy. Both paths are precious. Both belong to God. The mistake is to compare, measure, or rank them.

The writer offers a wisdom that remains deeply relevant today: do not spend your energy trying to determine why God leads one person differently than another.

That knowledge belongs to God.

Your task is simply to respond faithfully to the invitation that has been placed before you.

If you sense a deeper call toward contemplation, receive it with gratitude and humility. If you do not, then receive your present path with gratitude and humility. There is no need to envy another person's journey. Divine wisdom is shaping each soul according to a love far greater than human understanding.

And perhaps one of the greatest lessons of contemplation is learning not to tell God how He should work.

The ego always wants control. It wants answers, certainty, and explanations. It wants to organize grace according to its own understanding. But the contemplative heart slowly learns another posture entirely.

Trust.

God knows how to guide the soul.

God knows when to draw it deeper.

God knows what it needs.

And so the heart relaxes its grip. It ceases demanding. It stops comparing. It learns to walk the path before it with quiet faith, trusting that Love itself is leading the way.
