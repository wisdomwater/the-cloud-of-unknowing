## 77 — The Freedom of a Heart Fixed on God

> *When the heart is centered in God, everything else finds its proper place.*

The contemplative life often leads us into a deeper simplicity than we first imagined. At the beginning of the journey, we may assume that spiritual growth requires mastering complex techniques, acquiring special knowledge, or carefully managing every aspect of our inner life. Yet over time, the path becomes surprisingly simple.

Again and again, the soul is invited to return to love.

The author speaks of aspirations—those brief movements of the heart toward God that arise from a deeper place than ordinary thought. These movements do not depend upon elaborate reasoning or complicated methods. They emerge from the hidden center of the soul, from that place where grace quietly draws us toward God.

The mystics used many images to describe this hidden center. Some spoke of the height of the spirit. Others spoke of its depth, its ground, its center, or its innermost chamber. These are not different places but different ways of pointing toward the same mystery.

The soul has a depth that cannot be measured.

A stillness beneath thought.

A silence beneath words.

A place where God waits.

When prayer arises from this depth, even the smallest movement of love carries remarkable power. A brief turning of the heart may contain more sincerity than many words. A simple act of surrender may accomplish more than long efforts driven by anxiety or self-will.

This is why the author emphasizes freedom. Earlier in the book he suggested short prayer words such as "God," "Love," or "Sin." Yet here he carefully loosens any attachment to the method itself. No particular word is required. No specific formula is necessary. Some souls are helped by a sacred word. Others find themselves drawn into silence. Some pray inwardly. Others whisper their prayer aloud.

The form matters far less than the love.

God is not measuring syllables.

God is receiving the heart.

The contemplative tradition has always recognized this freedom. At times a prayer may rise silently within. At other times it may overflow into words, tears, gestures, singing, or expressions of joy. The outward form varies from person to person.

What matters is the source.

Does it arise from love?

Does it draw the soul toward God?

Does it foster humility, peace, and surrender?

If so, the form itself is secondary.

Yet the author offers an important caution. Not every emotional impulse is a movement of the Spirit. Especially in the early stages of the journey, strong emotions can easily be mistaken for spiritual depth. This is why humility and patience are essential companions on the contemplative path.

The deepest movements of grace often appear remarkably ordinary.

Quiet.

Gentle.

Unforced.

Like a river flowing beneath the surface.

The author then turns to an important truth about perseverance. We cannot remain in active prayer every moment of every day. Our bodies need food, rest, work, relationships, and countless ordinary activities. Yet even when we are not consciously praying, the heart can remain oriented toward God.

There is a difference between an act of love and a life of love.

The act may come and go.

The orientation remains.

The soul gradually develops a settled desire for God that persists beneath changing circumstances. Prayer becomes less like a task we perform and more like the direction in which our life is moving.

This brings us to one of the most beautiful insights in the chapter: true discretion is born from love.

Many people spend enormous energy trying to perfectly manage every detail of life. They worry about whether they are doing too much or too little, eating too much or too little, working too much or too little, praying too much or too little. While some reflection is necessary, excessive self-monitoring can become its own distraction.

The author suggests a different approach.

Seek God first.

Love God first.

Let the heart remain turned toward Him.

As love matures, wisdom gradually follows.

The soul becomes more balanced, not because it is obsessively controlling itself, but because God is quietly teaching it how to live.

This does not remove the need for responsibility. It simply places responsibility in its proper context. The goal is not perfect self-management. The goal is communion with God.

When that communion becomes the center of life, many other things begin to find their proper place naturally.

The heart becomes less anxious.

Less divided.

Less burdened by endless self-concern.

And little by little, a deeper freedom begins to emerge.

The freedom of a soul that has learned where its true home is.
