## 67 — Faithfulness in the Dark

> *Even when you cannot feel love, love may still be quietly at work within you.*

There are seasons in the spiritual life when everything seems to grow strangely quiet. The prayers that once flowed easily become dry. The sense of God's nearness fades. Even the ability to turn the heart toward Him may feel diminished. A person who once found comfort in prayer may suddenly discover only emptiness, weakness, and silence.

The mystics called this desolation.

Yet desolation is not the same as abandonment.

Often it is precisely in these seasons that God is doing some of His deepest work.

The soul may feel incapable of loving, incapable of praying, incapable even of seeking God as it once did. Yet beneath the surface, something hidden remains. The heart continues to choose faithfulness. It continues to endure. It continues to offer itself to God even when nothing seems to be happening. What appears from the outside to be inactivity may conceal a profound work of surrender.

In these seasons, love often takes a different form. Earlier in the journey, love may feel warm, joyful, expressive, and full of movement. During desolation, love becomes quieter. It reveals itself through patience, trust, perseverance, and willingness to remain present without reward. The soul discovers that love is not merely a feeling but a fidelity of the heart.

This is why the ancient writers sometimes viewed such seasons as a hidden purification. When consolations disappear, many of the subtle motives mixed into our devotion become visible. We begin to notice how much we depended on spiritual satisfaction, emotional reassurance, or a sense of progress. As these supports are removed, the heart is invited to love God for God's sake alone.

Such seasons are rarely pleasant. They can feel confusing, lonely, and deeply vulnerable. Yet they often become thresholds into greater freedom. The soul learns to remain with God not because prayer feels rewarding, but because God remains worthy of love even when nothing is felt in return.

The author suggests that beneath this apparent inactivity there may still be a secret movement of grace. The soul itself may not perceive it. It may believe it is accomplishing nothing at all. Yet God sees what the soul cannot see. He sees the hidden trust, the quiet surrender, and the steadfast willingness to remain turned toward Him in darkness.

As the contemplative life matures, prayer often becomes increasingly subtle. The soul no longer needs to constantly evaluate what is happening within. It learns to trust the deeper work of grace. Just as a healthy eye does not need to think about seeing, the mature soul gradually learns to rest in God's guidance without endless self-examination.

This simplicity cannot be manufactured. It grows slowly through years of surrender, struggle, trust, and love. The saints often spoke very little about these deepest movements of the soul, not because they wished to keep secrets, but because the realities themselves transcend language. Some experiences belong to a region beyond concepts, where words become too small for what is being encountered.

The contemplative path eventually leads into this holy simplicity. The soul ceases trying to control every step. It becomes more willing to be led than to lead. More willing to receive than to grasp.

And sometimes the deepest prayer is simply remaining available to God.

The next stage of this journey involves what the author calls abstraction. Yet this does not mean escaping the world or denying our humanity. It is a gradual loosening of the soul's attachment to itself, a growing freedom from constant self-preoccupation. Little by little, attention shifts away from the endless concern with "me" and rests more fully in the presence of God.

The path is long.

The transformation is gradual.

But every act of faithfulness, even in darkness, prepares the soul for greater freedom.
