## 25 — The Quiet Beauty of a Settled Soul

> *Love brings the soul into harmony.*
>
> *And what becomes peaceful within*
>
> *slowly softens outwardly too.*

There is a kind of spirituality that makes people more tense.

More rigid.

More performative.

More consumed with appearing holy than becoming whole.

The ancient contemplatives recognized this danger clearly. They observed how spiritual pride and inner instability often reveal themselves outwardly through restlessness, exaggeration, constant intensity, or the need to appear spiritually important.

But genuine contemplation moves differently.

It quiets the nervous system instead of inflaming it.

It humbles the ego instead of secretly feeding it.

It softens the body instead of filling it with constant strain.

The soul that slowly learns to rest in God often becomes more natural, not less.

More human.

More grounded.

There is something deeply healing about being in the presence of a person who no longer feels the need to prove themselves spiritually. Their peace does not come from performance. Their gentleness is not forced. Their humility feels unselfconscious.

You simply feel safer around them.

More able to breathe.

More able to become honest yourself.

The ancient writer suggests that true contemplative prayer gradually creates a kind of hidden beauty within a person. Not beauty in the worldly sense. Not perfection of appearance. But a quiet attractiveness born from inward harmony.

And many people have encountered this before without knowing how to describe it.

Certain people carry a calming presence.

Their speech is slower.

Their eyes feel kinder.

Their body no longer radiates frantic striving.

Even their silence feels welcoming rather than cold.

This kind of presence cannot be manufactured.

It emerges slowly through surrender.

Through humility.

Through honest prayer.

Through learning how to stop fighting yourself quite so constantly.

The contemplative path is not about becoming emotionally flat or artificially serene. Human beings will still feel grief, fear, exhaustion, longing, confusion, and joy. But something deeper begins stabilizing beneath those changing emotions.

The soul becomes less reactive.

Less consumed with self-display.

Less desperate for attention or validation.

And because of this, the body itself often begins carrying greater ease.

This matters more than people realize.

The body remembers what the soul repeatedly practices.

If your spirituality is rooted in fear, your body often learns tension.

If your spirituality is rooted in performance, your body often learns exhaustion.

If your spirituality is rooted in striving, your nervous system often learns pressure.

But when prayer becomes rooted in love, trust, honesty, and quiet surrender, the whole person slowly begins healing together.

Body.

Mind.

Soul.

The ancient contemplatives were not inviting people into spiritual theatrics.

They were inviting them into wholeness.

Into integrity.

Into a quieter relationship with themselves and with God.

And perhaps this is why the deepest saints so often appear ordinary on the surface. They do not need to constantly announce their spirituality. They are not obsessed with appearing mystical. Their peace has become woven gently into daily life.

Into how they listen.

How they speak.

How they move through suffering.

How they hold another person’s pain.

How they sit quietly in a room without needing to dominate it.

This is the hidden fruit of contemplation.

Not spectacle.

Not superiority.

But a heart becoming slowly governed by Love.

And wherever Love quietly governs the soul, beauty begins appearing there too.

## Breath Prayer

> Breath in: *Let Your peace settle within me*
>
> Breath out: *Shape me through quiet Love*
