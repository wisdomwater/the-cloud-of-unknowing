## 52 — Knowing Through Unknowing

> *When every lesser knowing falls away, love remains awake.*

The journey into divine mystery is not a movement toward greater certainty, but toward greater surrender.

The ancient story of Moses offers a beautiful image of this path. First he undergoes purification. Then he witnesses signs, voices, and radiant light. Step by step he ascends the holy mountain. Yet even after all these sacred experiences, he has not yet arrived at the deepest encounter.

There is still another invitation.

Beyond the voices.

Beyond the visions.

Beyond the light itself.

God calls Moses into the cloud.

This cloud represents something essential in the contemplative life. It is the place where familiar ways of knowing begin to fall silent. The soul can no longer rely upon understanding, imagination, or spiritual experiences. What once felt solid becomes uncertain. What once seemed clear becomes hidden.

Yet this darkness is not emptiness.

It is mystery.

The ancient writer says that Moses did not finally encounter God through sight or understanding, but through entering the darkness of unknowing. There he became free from both the things he could perceive and the powers by which he perceived them. He stood before a reality that could not be grasped by thought.

And still, somehow, he knew.

Not with the mind.

But with the whole of his being.

This is one of the great paradoxes of contemplative prayer. The closer we draw to God, the more we discover that God cannot be contained by our concepts. The intellect reaches its limit. The imagination grows quiet. Yet love continues onward.

The soul begins to know by participation rather than analysis.

It knows by presence rather than explanation.

It knows by communion rather than possession.

In this state, we are not empty because we lack knowledge. We are empty because we have released our need to control knowledge. We no longer seek to hold God within our understanding. Instead, we allow ourselves to be held within God's mystery.

The ancient writer says that by knowing nothing, the soul comes to know beyond the mind.

This does not mean becoming ignorant. It means moving beyond the illusion that ultimate reality can be reduced to thoughts and definitions. There is a wisdom that can only be received when the soul becomes quiet enough to listen.

And so we pray to enter this sacred darkness.

Not because darkness itself is holy, but because beyond the reach of ordinary sight, God waits. In the silence where certainty fades, another kind of vision begins to awaken. In the stillness where explanations fail, a deeper understanding emerges.

The soul discovers that not seeing can become a form of seeing.

Not knowing can become a form of knowing.

Silence can become a form of praise.

For God is not only beyond our words.

God is beyond the very categories by which words are formed.

And yet He remains closer than our own breath.

The ancient writer offers another image. Imagine a sculptor standing before a block of stone. The image is already hidden within it. The sculptor does not create the figure by adding something new. He reveals it by removing everything that conceals it.

So it is with the contemplative life.

The work is often one of subtraction rather than addition. We gently release attachments, assumptions, fears, images, and expectations. We loosen our grip on what obscures our vision. Little by little, what is false falls away.

What remains is not something we have constructed.

It is something that has always been there.

The Presence beneath every thought.

The Love beneath every longing.

The Mystery beneath every name.

And as these coverings are gradually removed, the soul begins to discover that God was never absent. He was simply hidden beneath all the things we thought we needed in order to find Him.
