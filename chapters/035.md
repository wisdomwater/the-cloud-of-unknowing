## 35 — Holy Desire Never Truly Leaves

> *The longing for God is itself a quiet sign that grace is already near.*

The contemplative path cannot be forced into existence through effort, intelligence, or spiritual ambition. It cannot be manufactured through technique or sustained by emotional intensity alone. At its deepest level, contemplation is always response — a response to grace already moving quietly within the soul long before the mind fully understands what is happening.

Because of this, the ancient writer offers something deeply comforting: not every person is drawn into contemplation in the same way. Some may admire the contemplative life from a distance without feeling inwardly called toward it. Others discover within themselves a persistent longing for silence, stillness, and loving awareness that continues returning throughout the years, even through seasons of distraction, dryness, or doubt.

This quiet returning matters deeply. The writer describes it almost like a hidden fire beneath the surface of the soul — a small but enduring desire for God that refuses to disappear completely. Not dramatic mystical experiences. Not constant emotional certainty. Simply longing. A gentle ache for deeper communion that quietly rises again and again within the heart. And this longing itself becomes one of the signs that grace is already at work.

The writer speaks carefully here because the human mind can easily confuse fascination with calling. Sometimes people become interested in contemplative spirituality because it feels unusual, poetic, mysterious, or intellectually beautiful. But contemplation is not spiritual entertainment. It asks something far more vulnerable from the soul.

It asks for surrender. For patience. For honesty. For the willingness to remain before God even when no immediate comfort arrives.

And it is often here that the deeper transformation begins. For every contemplative path eventually passes through seasons when the felt nearness of God seems to fade. Prayer becomes dry. Silence feels empty. The heart that once felt warm and alive now feels distracted, distant, or weary. Many people become frightened during these seasons. They quietly assume they have failed spiritually, or that God has withdrawn in disappointment.

Sometimes grace withdraws sweetness for a season so the soul does not become dependent upon spiritual feelings alone. Sometimes the silence deepens longing. Sometimes absence stretches the heart wider so it can receive more deeply later.

Even the loss becomes part of the preparation. And strangely, when the awareness of God’s nearness eventually returns, it is often received with far greater humility, tenderness, and gratitude than before. The soul slowly learns that contemplation is not about controlling spiritual experiences or preserving emotional intensity.

It is about consenting to relationship. And love matures slowly. Not through constant emotional certainty. But through faithfulness across changing seasons.

This is true of every deep relationship. There are moments of closeness and moments of silence. Moments of joy and moments of uncertainty. Yet beneath all these shifting experiences, love continues quietly growing in hidden ways that cannot always be measured from the surface.

The writer says something profoundly beautiful here: holy desire often grows stronger through delay. What is shallow usually fades when fulfillment is postponed. But what is real deepens. Like roots pressing downward through winter soil. Like seeds quietly unfolding beneath the darkness long before anything visible appears above the ground.

The longing itself becomes part of the transformation. Over time, the soul begins realizing that even the ache for God is already a form of communion. The desire to love God more deeply is itself evidence that Love has already begun awakening within you.

And this realization softens something important inside the heart. You stop judging yourself so harshly. You stop demanding constant certainty from prayer. You stop measuring spiritual life by emotional intensity alone.

Instead, you begin trusting the quieter movements of grace — the returning, the longing, the willingness to begin again despite weakness, distraction, or uncertainty. Again and again.

The ancient writer closes with remarkable tenderness and humility. He does not present himself as someone who has mastered the mystery. He simply offers companionship along the way and entrusts the rest to God. And perhaps this is one of the deepest wisdoms hidden within the contemplative life:

The soul is not transformed through force. It is slowly ripened through love. Through holy desire. Through quiet returning. Through grace patiently drawing the heart toward deeper peace, even through seasons of darkness and delay.

And if the longing still remains within you — however faintly — then the journey is not over. Love has not abandoned you. Rather, it is holding you is ways you too great for you to know fully.

And in this unknowing you are truly known.
