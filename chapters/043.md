## 43 — The Cross of the Self

> *The deepest surrender is not the loss of life, but the loosening of self.*

There comes a painful season in the contemplative life when the soul begins realizing that even its own self-awareness can feel like a veil between itself and God.

At first, this can sound unsettling. But the ancient writer is not speaking about rejecting existence or despising the goodness of being human. He is describing the strange sorrow that arises when the heart has tasted something of divine love and longs for fuller union still. The soul begins yearning to rest so completely in God that even its constant preoccupation with itself starts to feel heavy.

And this burden can become very painful. You begin noticing how continually the mind returns to itself—its emotions, fears, spiritual progress, failures, desires, memories, and inner noise. Even in prayer, the self often remains at the center. The contemplative path slowly exposes this hidden gravity within us, not to shame us, but to free us.

The writer speaks fiercely here because the work of surrender reaches very deep. The soul does not seek to “cease existing.” That would be despair, not love. Rather, it longs to be released from its endless fixation upon itself so it may rest more fully in God. It begins fleeing self-absorption the way one flees poison—not because the self is evil, but because love desires something larger than the prison of constant self-reference. And yet, this surrender is not easily accomplished.

Even when you sincerely desire to forget yourself in God, the awareness of your own being continues following you. The mind keeps returning. The ego keeps speaking. The inner world keeps pressing itself between the soul and the quiet simplicity of divine presence.

This is where the true cross begins. Not merely outward suffering. Not merely difficult circumstances. But the painful awareness of the self that cannot fully free itself by its own strength.

The soul discovers how restless it truly is. How deeply it clings to identity, control, and self-consciousness. And for a time, this can feel unbearably heavy. The ancient writer cries out almost tenderly here: “Jesu help thee then.” For this inner burden can wound more deeply than many outward sorrows.

Yet even this pain becomes part of the path. Christ’s invitation to carry the cross is not only about enduring hardship in the world. It is also about bearing patiently the slow purification of the heart. The soul carries itself before God—the noise, the resistance, the limitations, the endless returning inward—and learns not to despair.

Love keeps reaching. Grace keeps working. And over time, the burden that once felt unbearable begins softening into surrender.

Then the soul starts discovering a quiet sweetness hidden beneath the struggle. Not constant ecstasy. Not emotional intensity. But moments of deep inward stillness where God’s presence feels nearer than the self itself. The soul tastes, even briefly, the gentleness of divine love carrying what it could never heal alone.

The writer then turns to an important warning: contemplation is not built merely upon clever thoughts, spiritual imagination, or emotional meditations.

Even holy reflections can only carry the soul so far. Thinking about God is not yet the same as resting in God. And yet, the path into contemplation often begins through these sacred meditations. Reflecting on Christ’s life, suffering, mercy, and love awakens the heart. Meditation softens the inner world and opens the door toward deeper prayer. The imagination, when used humbly, becomes a servant leading the soul toward love. But eventually, love calls the soul beyond images and concepts into direct, silent presence.

The writer beautifully describes Christ as both the doorway and the guide. Through the humanity of Jesus, we are invited into the mystery of God Himself. We enter through compassion—through sorrow for the suffering we have caused, through gratitude for divine mercy, and through wonder that Love would come so near to us in human form.

Contemplation never bypasses Christ. It passes through Him. Through His tenderness. Through His humility. Through His willingness to enter human suffering so completely that no wounded soul would ever need to walk alone again.

And so the contemplative life remains deeply grounded in love made visible. Not escape from humanity. But the healing of humanity through union with Divine Love itself.
