## 24 — When Spirituality Becomes Strain

> *God is not found*
>
> *through inner violence.*
>
> *Love moves more gently than that.*

One of the quiet dangers on the spiritual path is the temptation to force mystical experience.

The longing for God is beautiful. The desire to go deeper is holy. But when this longing becomes entangled with fear, ambition, obsession, or spiritual insecurity, the soul can begin treating contemplation as something to conquer rather than receive.

The ancient contemplatives speak very carefully about this.

For there is a difference between quiet inward prayer and unhealthy inward fixation.

Some people become so determined to “experience God” that they begin turning all their attention inward with intense strain. They analyze every sensation. They force concentration. They become hyperaware of bodily feelings, emotions, thoughts, and mental imagery.

And eventually the nervous system becomes overwhelmed.

The imagination grows overheated.

The boundary between genuine spiritual awareness and psychological projection begins to blur.

This does not make someone evil.

It makes them human.

Especially wounded humans.

Especially anxious humans.

Especially people who desperately long for certainty, safety, transcendence, or relief from pain.

The contemplative tradition understands this with surprising tenderness. It recognizes that the mind can become confused when spirituality loses groundedness. A person may begin mistaking emotional intensity, bodily sensations, inner imagery, or unusual psychological states for divine revelation.

And once this happens, discernment becomes difficult.

The person may feel deeply spiritual while slowly drifting away from reality, humility, simplicity, and embodied peace.

This is why true contemplation always carries a certain steadiness.

A certain sobriety.

A certain softness.

The deeper a person moves into genuine prayer, the less frantic they often become. Their presence grows quieter. More grounded. More spacious. They no longer need to force extraordinary experiences in order to feel close to God.

Love itself becomes enough.

The ancient writer describes how strained spirituality can eventually distort both mind and body. The person becomes restless, agitated, emotionally inflated, overly dramatic, or psychologically consumed with spiritual experience. Their body carries the tension of inner striving. Their nervous system loses ease.

And though the medieval language is sharp at times, underneath it is actually a compassionate warning:

Do not harm yourself trying to grasp what can only be received gently.

This remains deeply relevant today.

Modern people are often exhausted before they even begin contemplative practice. Many carry chronic anxiety, trauma, nervous-system dysregulation, loneliness, religious fear, or emotional fragmentation. And when spirituality becomes another arena of pressure and self-judgment, the soul grows even more burdened.

But contemplation was never meant to fracture you.

It was meant to soften you.

Not through passivity.

Not through avoidance.

But through quiet honesty before God.

True prayer gradually gathers the scattered parts of the self into greater peace. It does not demand constant intensity. It does not glorify exhaustion. It does not require dramatic emotional states in order to be real.

The soul learns instead how to rest.

How to breathe.

How to become present without force.

And this inward peace slowly begins expressing itself outwardly as well.

The ancient writer makes a surprisingly beautiful observation here: genuine contemplative prayer changes not only the inner life, but also a person’s outward presence. The body itself becomes calmer. The face softens. The movements become less frantic. Speech becomes gentler. The whole person begins carrying a quieter kind of harmony.

Not perfection.

Not artificial serenity.

But a certain groundedness born from living less divided within yourself.

For love naturally shapes the body that carries it.

A peaceful heart often creates a more peaceful presence.

And perhaps this is one of the simplest ways to discern whether spirituality is becoming healthy within you:

Does it make you softer?

More patient?

More honest?

More grounded?

More able to love?

Or does it leave you increasingly agitated, inflated, disconnected, performative, fearful, or consumed with yourself?

The contemplative path does not lead away from humanity.

It leads more deeply into wholeness.

And there, beneath all the striving, God waits patiently — not demanding spectacle, but inviting you gently into rest.

## Breath Prayer

> Breath in: *Quiet my striving heart*
>
> Breath out: *Teach me gentle rest*
