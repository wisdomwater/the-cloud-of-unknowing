## 24 — When Spirituality Becomes Strain

> *God is not reached through inner violence, but through gentle surrender.*

One of the quiet dangers along the contemplative path is the temptation to force what can only be received. The longing for God is beautiful, and the desire to go deeper into prayer is holy. But when this longing becomes tangled with fear, insecurity, ambition, or desperation, the soul can slowly begin treating contemplation as something to conquer rather than something to enter with humility and trust.

The ancient contemplatives speak carefully about this because they understood how easily sincere spirituality can become strained. There is a difference between quiet inward prayer and unhealthy inward fixation. A person may become so determined to “experience God” that they turn inward with excessive intensity, analyzing every sensation, forcing concentration, and becoming hyperaware of thoughts, emotions, bodily feelings, or inner imagery.

And eventually, the nervous system begins to carry the burden of that striving. The imagination grows overheated. The boundary between genuine spiritual awareness and psychological projection becomes increasingly difficult to recognize.

This does not make someone foolish or evil. It makes them human. Especially wounded humans. Especially anxious humans. Especially people quietly carrying deep longing for certainty, safety, transcendence, or relief from suffering.

The contemplative tradition understands this with far more tenderness than many people realize. The old writers knew that the mind can become confused when spirituality loses groundedness. Emotional intensity, vivid imagination, bodily sensations, or unusual inner experiences can slowly begin to feel like divine revelation, even when they are simply the nervous system struggling beneath strain and longing. And once this confusion deepens, discernment becomes difficult.

A person may feel profoundly spiritual while quietly drifting further away from humility, simplicity, embodiment, and peace.

This is why genuine contemplation carries a certain steadiness within it. The deeper a soul moves into true prayer, the less frantic it often becomes. There is usually more softness, more groundedness, more spaciousness. The need to manufacture extraordinary experiences slowly begins to loosen because love itself becomes enough.

Not spectacle. Not emotional intensity. Not constant spiritual excitement. Simply love.

The ancient writer observed that strained spirituality eventually affects both mind and body. The person becomes restless, emotionally inflated, overly dramatic, or inwardly consumed with spiritual experiences. Their body begins carrying the tension of constant striving. Their nervous system loses its ease.

And beneath the severe language the old writers sometimes used, there is actually a compassionate warning hidden there: Do not harm yourself trying to grasp what can only be received gently.

This wisdom remains deeply needed today. Many modern people are already exhausted before they ever begin contemplative practice. They carry anxiety, trauma, chronic stress, loneliness, religious fear, emotional fragmentation, or nervous-system exhaustion within their bodies. And when spirituality becomes another arena of pressure, performance, or self-judgment, the soul grows even more burdened.

But contemplation was never meant to fracture you. It was meant to soften you. Not through avoidance or passivity, but through honest presence before God. True prayer slowly gathers the scattered parts of the self into greater peace. It does not demand constant intensity. It does not glorify exhaustion. It does not require dramatic emotional experiences in order to be real. Instead, the soul gradually learns how to rest. How to breathe again. How to become present without force.

And as this inward peace deepens, it often begins expressing itself outwardly as well. The ancient contemplatives noticed something surprisingly beautiful: genuine prayer changes not only the inner life, but also the outward presence of a person. The body itself often becomes calmer. The face softens. Speech grows gentler. Movements become less frantic and hurried. The whole person begins carrying a quieter kind of harmony.

Not perfection. Not artificial serenity. But a groundedness born from becoming less divided within yourself.

For love naturally shapes the body that carries it. A peaceful heart often creates a more peaceful presence. And perhaps this becomes one of the simplest ways of discerning whether spirituality is growing in a healthy direction within you.

Does it make you softer? More patient? More honest? More grounded? More able to love others gently?

Or does it leave you increasingly agitated, performative, fearful, inflated, disconnected, or consumed with yourself?

The contemplative path does not lead away from your humanity. It leads more deeply into wholeness. And there, beneath all the striving and exhaustion, God waits patiently for you — not demanding spectacle or perfection, but inviting you quietly into rest.

### Breath Prayer

> **Inhale:** *Quiet my striving heart*
>
> **Exhale:** *Teach me gentle rest*
